# Abstract 

The optimal control problem is a significant open problem in legged robot agile locomotion. Model Predictive Control (MPC) is an extremely powerful control framework for complex systems. However, when using popular approaches such as Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI), they fail because of the reliance on unstructured, isotropic noise for exploration, which is problematic in environments with complex reward landscapes. This work first extends the state-of-the-art DIAL-MPC by adding temporally correlated noise via low-pass, colored, and band-limited filters to see if this improves control smoothness. The main contribution is the introduction of VIGAS, or Variational Inference Guided Annealing Search, a new generative MPC algorithm that uses an adaptive, low-rank plus diagonal Gaussian variational distribution instead of unstructured sampling. VIGAS learns about the underlying structure of a reward landscape online by adapting its parameters via a natural gradient step using a reward-weighted SVD. By adopting an implicit annealing approach, VIGAS can then allocate more exploration towards promising areas of the control space. In the Brax physics simulator, we performed a comparative analysis showing VIGAS consistently outperformed MPPI, DIAL-MPC, and DIAL-MPC plus filters. VIGAS had higher cumulative rewards but also performed better (or at worst, comparable) in a variety of additional metrics including walk tracking error, average total contact reward for sequential jumping, and success rate for crate climbing. These extensive results, evaluated across many diverse and complex robotic locomotion tasks, showcase that adaptive, structured exploration is an effective way to tackle difficult control problems. v

# Chapter 1 

# Introduction 

Attaining stable and agile locomotion is a major challenge in robotics, and is often viewed as a core example of embodied intelligence and a condition for physical auton-omy. Legged robots, in particular, constitute the challenge of ultimate partitioning the distance between their biological relative agents and their machine cousins that will be able to traverse environments that have the complexity and variation that they are able to navigate with high efficiency and agility. The ability to dash, leap, and change directions quickly is important, whether for first responders on hazardous terrains, search-and-rescue missions, planetary exploration, industrial automation, and so on. Attaining such motions embodying dynamic maneuverability is still no easy task. The dynamics of legged systems have unstable, high-dimensional system dynamics and hybrid phenomena like intermittent contacts with the ground input that are entirely baffling as they relate to traditional stabilization approaches in control. Model Predictive Control (MPC) has quickly evolved and emerged as a key means for addressing such complicated control tasks [ 10 ]. MPC achieves this by iteratively solving an optimal control problem over a finite, receding horizon, producing reactive and dynamically feasible motions while respecting physical system constraints. In recent years, sampling-based "shooting" methods for MPC, which sample the space and evaluate the consequences of many candidate action sequences, have garnered attention. These approaches build on foundational theories from stochastic optimal control (e.g., [ 13 , 26 ]) and relate to solving arbitrary cost functions and non-linear dynamics in robotics. For example, Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI) control uses stochastic samplers around nominal trajectories while fusing the results by providing a reward-weighted averaging [ 30 ] scheme. Despite its performance, MPPI inherently restricts the control to its exploration strategy of sampling unstructured, isotropic Gaussian noise. This type of exploration is very sample inefficient, typically requiring thousands of rollouts per time step to find acceptable solutions, especially in reward landscapes with narrow optima or deceptive local minima. Recently developed methods such as DIAL-MPC have attempted to improve on this issue with a planned annealing schedule, which iteratively refines the control plan, in a manner which resembles a diffusion-denoising process [ 31 ]. Although DIAL-MPC demonstrates improvements in performance, the exploration process with DIAL-MPC is still being fundamentally limited to a predefined noise schedule, 2 1. Introduction 

rather than being adapted to the actual structure of the reward landscape for the task. This brings us to our core problem statement: the existing stochastic MPC methods lack an effective exploration strategy which is adapted to the reward landscape of the task, given the inherent sample complexity of the approach, this results in spatio-temporally unoptimized performance on agile locomotion tasks. This work sets out to overcome this limitation in the existing literature with three main contributions. First, we extend the DIAL-MPC baseline with temporally correlated noise structures using low-pass, colored, and band-limited filters. This design decision aims to improve the smoothness of the control and lead to more physically plausible exploration trajectories. Second, and most importantly, we introduce VIGAS (Variational Inference Guided Annealing Search) . VIGAS is the first algorithm to conceptualize the MPC exploration problem for agile locomotion as a variational inference problem. VIGAS uses an adaptive low-rank plus diagonal Gaussian distribution as a posterior over optimal control sequences, providing a new and powerful bridge between these two paradigms. The parameters of this distribution are updated by fitting its moments to reward-weighted samples, with a Singular Value Decomposition (SVD), that efficiently determines the covariance structure and performs implicit annealing of the search. Finally, we detail a rigorous evaluation and comparison within the Brax physics simulator [ 9 ], focusing on the performance of VIGAS against MPPI, the original DIAL-MPC, and DIAL-MPC with our filter enhancements across multiple challenging locomotion tasks. Overall, our findings show that by following its own adaptive exploration strategy, VIGAS statistically outperforms the baseline methods in this study; netting higher cumulative rewards, maintaining smooth control profiles, and significantly fewer control samples. Additionally, on three key, task-specific metrics (walk-tracking error, average total contact reward during sequential jumping; and the success rate climbing on crates) VIGAS either outperformed or achieved parity. These results demonstrate profound outcomes through utilizing structured adaptive exploration to achieve better efficiency and performance from the MPC algorithm for robotics. The thesis is structured as follows. Chapter 2 provides a theoretical background on Model Predictive Control, stochastic optimal control, and variational inference. Chapter 3 details the baseline algorithms (e.g., MPPI and DIAL-MPC) and our proposed filter-based extensions. Chapter 4 details the formulation and implementa-tion of our proposed VIGAS algorithm. Chapter 5 describes the experimental setup (i.e., the simulation environments, tasks, evaluation metrics). Chapter 6 details and analyzes the comparison results of our experiments. Chapter 7 outlines the implications of these results, the limitations of the work provided herein, and future lines of work for the research. 3

Although importance sampling is an efficient sampling method, its efficiency is largely based on the proposal distribution. As mentioned in Section 2.2, algorithms like MPPI are using a simple Gaussian centered on the last solution, which may be a poor approximation of the true optimal distribution. If the proposal doesn’t resemble the target very much, many samples will be "wasted" in the low reward areas. Variational Inference (VI) provides a principled way to learn a better proposal distribution online [4].

To minimize this KL divergence is equivalent to maximizing the Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO), which is the tractable objective. For sampling-based approaches, this optimization problem can be solved iteratively. In this loop, we: (1) sample from the current distribution qθold , (2) use the rewards to derive importance weights, and (3) update the parameters θ of qθ to match the weighted samples. By iterating this loop in combination, we are refining our approximation at each iteration.

The choice of the variational family qθ is of key importance. The normal distribution,

Σ = LL T + diag (d). (2.5) In this expression, L is a D × k matrix (which has rank k ≪ D), and d is a D-dimensional vector. The power of this decomposition is that the matrix L contains the k most dominant correlation patterns in the control space (for example, the temporal dependency between joint torques) and the vector d simply models the variance remaining across the diagonal. # 3.2 Baseline 2: DIAL-MPC 

DIAL-MPC (Diffusion-Inspired Annealing for Legged MPC) is a state-of-the-art, training-free MPC process with a number of advantages over MPPI, which is the establishment of a more structured exploration and refinement process [ 31 ]. The inspiration for the algorithm is based on a relevant theoretical perspective which relates MPPI to the mathematics of score-based diffusion models. 3.2 Baseline 2: DIAL-MPC 9

3.2.1 Theoretical Motivation: MPPI as a Single-Stage Diffusion 

At the start of analyzing MPPI, the authors of DIAL-MPC framed the optimal control problem as optimizing over the mode of a target probability distribution as 

p0(U) ∝ exp (−J(U)/λ ), where J(U) is the trajectory cost. The authors indicate that the standard MPPI update rule (Equation 4.1) is equivalent to performing a single gradient ascent step on the log-likelihood of a "diffused" distribution p1(U)

that is created by convolving the target density with a Gaussian sampling kernel 

ϕ(·) = N (0, Σ). In particular, they show that the MPPI update can be represented as a score-matching step: 

Unew ≈ Uold + Σ · ∇ log p1(Uold ), (3.1) where ∇ log p1(U) is the score function (the gradients of the log probability) of the diffused distribution. The key takeaway is that MPPI doesn’t optimize the true objective landscape but rather a smoothed version of it. Though smoothing actions with noise may help the optimization process, fixing the noise level (i.e., fixing Σ)provides a fundamental trade-off that occurs: the larger the noise (variance), the more local minima we avoid, but with a larger bias; the smaller the noise (variance), the smaller the bias but the more likely we will get stuck. 

3.2.2 The DIAL-MPC Algorithm: Multi-Stage Annealing 

DIAL-MPC makes use of this trade-off by using a multi-stage annealing process, moving away from managing the single-stage diffusion propositions of MPPI inspired by denoising diffusion models [ 12 ]. Rather than performing a single update, DIAL-MPC combines multiple updates that result in a series of updates per MPC time-step that iteratively smooth and decrease the variance of the sampling noise. Thus, the optimization process establishes a heavily smoothed landscape for robustly exploring a global optimum and transitions into a less biased landscape for precisely exploiting a closer, local optimum. This process is formalized with a dual-loop annealing process. 

Trajectory-Level Annealing (Outer Loop). This is the main iterative loop of the algorithm, detailed in Algorithm 2. The optimization happens over Ndiffuse steps. • Reward Normalization via Improvement Score: The reward processing performed is more clever than the standard MPPI. Before applying the softmax weights, the reward of each sampled trajectory is first evaluated against the reward value for the current unperturbed nominal trajectory, termed the improvement score. The improvement score is then normalized (standardized) to focus the optimization on samples that improved upon the current planning and made the optimization more robust to the absolute scaling of the reward function.

Ynom [0] . This anchors the search at the current state and ensures relevance to the imminent action. These mechanisms yield what the authors describe as "better global coverage and local convergence."

Implementation note (timing): In our implementation, the diffusion/anneal-ing loop is executed after by applying the first control and shifting the horizon at each MPC step; the refined plan is then used at the next step.

# 4.1 Conceptual Founda-tions and Positioning in the Literature 

In its most fundamental form, VIGAS is viewing exploration in MPC as a problem of learning an optimal sampling distribution in real time. Phrasing VIGAS in this manner puts it alongside a rich family of optimization methods in the ’black box’ or ’model based search,’ that have conceptual relationships to existing methods, but we are concerned with their application to the online context of receding horizon control. Theoretically, VIGAS is applying Natural Evolution Strategies (NES) 16 4. VIGAS: Variational Inference Guided Annealing Search 

[29 ] through trajectory optimization. In NES, a function is optimized by updating a search distribution’s parameters via natural gradient ascent on expected fitness iteratively. VIGAS exploits this idea by using a Gaussian search distribution over control sequences and updating the parameters based on the rewards from sampled trajectories. 

VIGAS vs. CMA-ES: It is natural to think of VIGAS as a type of Covariance Matrix Adaptation Evolution Strategy (CMA-ES) [27 ], which is one of the most well-studied algorithms for non-convex optimization. Both VIGAS and CMA-ES fit a full Gaussian distribution to the local topography of the objective function, but they differ in how they do that update and the types of problems they deal with. CMA-ES uses clever, path-dependent update rules for the covariance matrix (rank-one and rank-μ updates) that allow for reliable convergence to either a local or global minima in dynamic static optimization problems. VIGAS, on the other hand, uses a straightforward, reward-weighted resampling formulation to update the distribution, much like the Cross-Entropy Method (CEM) [22]. 

VIGAS vs. CEM: While both VIGAS and CEM use a "sample, weight, fit" loop, CEM typically uses a hard selection of ’elite’ samples to update its distribution parameters [ 22 , 19 ]. VIGAS used softmax weights for a soft selection that gives a continuous influence based on reward, which more aligns with a natural gradient perspective. In addition, VIGAS inherently learns a low-rank plus diagonal covariance structure via SVD on weighted samples, something often not an option in the typical CEM variant, which updates a full covariance matrix of elite samples without any such factorization [ 2]. This deliberate factorization and SVD-based update for a structured covariance is a critical differentiator for VIGAS and facilitates more efficient adaptation in high-dimensional control spaces. 

VIGAS vs. PI 2 and MPPI: VIGAS is clearly built on the foundation of path integral control. There are other methods that are also path integral based, such as PI 2 [24 ] and MPPI [ 30 ], that employ a reward-weighted average over multiple samples. However, in these methods, the search distribution is still updated simply with respect to the mean or nominal trajectory, while they either fix the covariance or simply adapt it in a heuristic fashion (including stuff like isotropic scaling of noise or trajectories); thus, they learn "where" to search but not "how" to search. VIGAS takes this a step further in evolving both the mean and covariance, which enables learning not just "where" to search but also "how" to search best. This is particularly important as the covariance parameterizes how we sample trajectories in the control space, thus allowing VIGAS to identify and exploit complex correlations in the optimal control space, which are lost when using a fixed covariance. 

VIGAS vs. Other Variational Inference MPCs (e.g., Tsallis VI-MPC): 

VIGAS is not the first to apply Variational Inference to the MPC problem. Some other methods exist, such as Tsallis VI-MPC [28 ], which also reformulate the problem using variational inference and develop sample weighted projections based on a Gaussian policy distribution. However, unlike VIGAS, Tsallis VI-MPC uses a 4.2 From Path Integral Control to Variational Inference 17 

full covariance matrix and relies directly on weighted sample covariance to derive the updated Gaussian policy feedback, without considering dimensionality reductions like low-rank approximations or SVD-based factorization for scale and efficiency. Naturally, this makes Tsallis VI-MPC less effective for the high-dimensional control problems, at which VIGAS excels. 

VIGAS vs. SLANG: Likewise, while based on low-rank plus diagonal covariance parameterizations and natural gradient using eigenvalue factorization, SLANG (Stochastic Low-rank Approximate Natural-Gradient) [17 ] is a method for uncertainty estimation under the auspices of Bayesian deep learning, while VIGAS is fundamentally an MPC algorithm aimed at stochastic trajectory optimization. SLANG performs updates based on back-propagated gradients, while VIGAS ex-plicitly performs updates based on trajectories that are weighted by the rewards obtained. Even though VIGAS and SLANG share some value of similar updates, the nature of the data and the domain of application render VIGAS a distinctive MPC approach compared to SLANG. 

VIGAS vs. MPOPI: More recently, MPOPI (Model Predictive Optimized Path Integral) [15 ] has also emerged to study legged robots, and is based upon principles of MPPI, CEM, and CMA-ES. While MPOPI also updates a Gaussian distribution’s mean and full covariance (again solely based on weighted samples), in applications for locomotion in an MPC context, it does not leverage a low-rank plus diagonal covariance structure, nor the SVD-based updates to covariance factorization that are sensitive to VIGAS’s efficiency, or the desired adaptive exploration in high dimensions, which is the hallmark of VIGAS. 

Key Differentiators of VIGAS: Notably, neither Tsallis VI-MPC, SLANG, nor MPOPI use the principled receding horizon shift of covariance factors 

(L and d), which is central to the operation of VIGAS, which is specifically and methodologically designed to benefit from execution in the context of continuous MPC. The receding horizon design of VIGAS ensures we are not simply learning correlations to exploit within each execution, but rather we are uniformly propagating the updates to the learned correlations across the receding horizon problem. All importantly, VIGAS also displays implicit annealing (i.e., covariance contracts naturally because we concentrate weights) instead of systematically and arbitrarily scheduling noise.

4.3.3 Connection to the Natural Gradient 

The reward-weighted MLE update is more than a heuristic and serves as an efficient implementation of an approximate natural gradient step [ 1]. A "vanilla" gradient update would optimize the expected reward with respect to the parameters θ =

{μ, L, d} directly, which can be slow and unstable as it does not account for the fact that small changes in θ can cause large changes in the distribution qθ. The natural gradient is correct for this because it operates in the space of distributions, along the most direct trajectory of "information distance" (KL-divergence). Fitting a new distribution to weighted samples is a natural gradient update for Gaussian distributions [ 18 ]. As such, performing the reward-weighted MLE means VIGAS is implicitly carrying out the natural gradient step while simultaneously avoiding the need to create and invert the Fisher Information Matrix, yielding a swift, stable, and theoretically grounded update.

# 4.5 Hyperparameter Analysis 

The behavior of VIGAS is governed by a set of crucial hyperparameters that control the trade-off between exploration, exploitation, and stability. 

• Rank ( k): The vigas_rank hyperparameter determines the expressiveness of the correlated component of the covariance. A higher rank k allows the model to capture more complex, multi-dimensional dependencies within the control sequence. However, this comes at the cost of increased computation for the SVD and a higher risk of overfitting to the samples of a single iteration, especially if the number of samples N is not sufficiently large relative to k.

Rank k and number of samples N. For any useful low-rank estimate we should have k ≤ min (D, N ); larger k will need more computing resources, and less N will increase risk of overfitting. 

• Temperature ( λ): The vigas_temp parameter controls the "greediness" of the sample selection by sharpening or flattening the softmax-derived weights. A low λ leads to a very selective, exploitative update focused on the best few samples, while a high λ results in a more explorative update by averaging over a wider range of samples. Its effect is most predictable when reward standardization is enabled. 

Temperature and standardization: λ acts after optional z-scoring of rewards; with standardization enabled, λ’s effect is invariant to reward scale. 

• EMA Factors ( αμ, α cov ): The vigas_mu_alpha and vigas_cov_alpha define learning rate for mean and covariance during the update expressed as a sort of soft trust region. For example, an aggressive update (1.0) means the new parameters are fully accepted, while lower values (close to 0.0) present stable and conservative updates, which may be too conservative. 

• Reward Standardization: The boolean flag vigas_standardize , when enabled, normalizes the rewards (zero mean, unit variance) before they are passed to the softmax function. This makes the effective influence of the temperature λ invariant to the absolute scale and offset of the task’s reward function, greatly improving robustness and simplifying hyperparameter tuning. 

• Variance Controls: Two parameters control the magnitude of the diagonal variance: 

– Initial Variance ( vigas_init_var ): Sets the breadth of isotropic ex-ploration at startup and is also used to re-initialize the variance for the final step of the receding horizon. 

– Covariance Floor ( vigas_cov_floor ): Provides a minimum value for the diagonal variance during the update, preventing the distribution from collapsing and ensuring a minimum level of exploration is always maintained. 25

# 5.1 Simulation Environment: Brax 

This work uses the Brax physics engine [ 9] to run all experiments. Brax is a differentiable, modern, high-performance physics simulator built in JAX [ 5 ]. We chose to use Brax based on two distinct advantages: 

• Just-In-Time (JIT) Compilation: Brax uses JAX’s compilation to compile all simulation rollouts into optimized XLA kernels that can be executed in parallel on modern CPUs and GPUs. This property is essential for sampling-based MPC, which requires simulating hundreds or thousands of rollouts simultaneously at each time step to perform in real-time. 

• Reproducibility: As a JAX-native library, Brax provides strong repro-ducibility guarantees through explicit pseudo-random number generation key management, which is critical for scientific comparison. Our entire implementation, including the MPC update loop, the rollouts, and the optimizer updates, is JIT-compiled, which is critical for achieving real-time performance.

5.2.1 The Control Interface: From MPC Actions to Motor Torques 

A critical aspect of our setup is the control interface. While the overall system performs full-order torque control, the MPC planner itself operates in a normalized, abstract action space a ∈ [−1, 1] nu , where nu is the number of actuated joints. This abstract action is translated into motor torques via a two-stage process embedded within the environment’s dynamics function: 1. Action Scaling: The normalized action at from the planner is first scaled to a target joint position, qtarget , within the robot’s feasible joint range. This mapping is performed by the act2joint function. 2. PD Control: The target joint position is then passed to a low-level Proportional-Derivative (PD) controller, which computes the final motor torque command 

τ t to be applied in the simulator. This is handled by the act2tau function and is defined as: 

τ t = Kp(qtarget − qt) + Kd(0 − ˙qt), (5.1) where qt and ˙qt are the current joint positions and velocities, and Kp and Kd

are diagonal matrices of proportional and derivative gains, respectively. Therefore, the MPC planner is effectively a high-level kinematic planner, and the low-level PD controller is treated as part of the system dynamics f (xt, ut) that the planner optimizes over. This setup is standard for applying sampling-based planners to torque-controlled robots and provides a realistic and challenging benchmark.

# Conclusion and Future Work 

This thesis set out to address a fundamental challenge in modern robotics: the development of efficient and high-performing motion controllers for agile legged locomotion. We determined that the fundamental limitations of the sampling-based state-of-the-art MPC methods were primarily their usage of fixed, non-adaptive exploration strategies. In this chapter, we will present our takeaways from this work, its implications for robotic control, our assessment of the limitations of our approach, and some promising future research directions. 

# 7.1 Primary Contributions 

The contributions of this thesis can be summarized in three key components. The first of these contributions was the design, implementation and evaluation of VIGAS (Variational Inference Guided Annealing Search) , which is a new, training-free MPC method that reformulates the control problem as a problem of online variational inference. VIGAS learns an adaptive, task-specific sampling distribution with a flexible but computationally inexpensive low-rank plus diagonal Gaussian model and learns its parameters in a SVD that is weighted by reward, with an algorithm that we have proven is a valid implementation of a natural gradient step in the space of probability distributions. The second contributions can be characterized by the enhancements to the DIAL-MPC baseline, namely we enhanced DIAL-MPC’s representation of temporally-correlated noise by replacing white noise with low-pass, colored, and band-limited filters. Most importantly, our extensive empirical results showed that adaptive ex-ploration strategies can achieve better performance and staggering improvements to efficiency, thereby breaking the typical trade-off between solution quality and computational cost that pervades the state of the art of sampling-based MPC. 

# 7.2 Theoretical and Practical Implications 

The success of VIGAS carries profound implications for both the theoretical under-standing and practical deployment of sampling-based control systems. 44 7. Conclusion and Future Work 

Paradigm Shift from Fixed to Adaptive Exploration. VIGAS embodies a deep paradigm shift from hand-designing exploration schedules to learning explo-ration strategies online. Existing approaches such as DIAL-MPC have clear and demonstrably effective annealing schedules, but are ultimately heuristic; VIGAS performs implicit, task-aware annealing : the variance of the learned distribution converges with exploration to high-reward regions, and the exploration-exploitation trade-off is given by the structure of the reward landscape, rather than a fixed annealing schedule. Our empirical convergence analysis has confirmed this adaptive behaviour, and VIGAS has distinct exploration and adaptation behaviours, which vary from task to task. VIGAS performs deeper search when it is useful, and converges rapidly once it has obtained sufficient information. This represents the first real-time MPC demonstration that: (i) is adaptive exploratory learning on-line, (ii) goes further than heuristics for collective motions with MPC, and approaches a meaningful, principled and data-driven prospect for exploration strategies. 

Linking Optimal Control to Modern Machine Learning. VIGAS brings together classical concepts from the optimal control space and modern generative data-driven approaches. In demonstrating MPC is an online variational inference problem, we have highlighted how concepts in probabilistic machine learning such as natural gradients, moment matching, structured covariance modelling etc., can be leveraged for robotic control in real time. The very strong theory describing reward-weighted SVD updates as closely linked to natural gradient steps, is one straightforward way to separate sampling-based control methods from heuristic approaches, and to move to mathematically grounded exploratory strategies. Exploration strategies of fundamental probabilistic structure offer many rich research directions at the junction of control theory research and machine learning practice, that can provide better ways to consider classical control methods. 

Practical Revolution in Computational Efficiency. The remarkable advances in efficiency demonstrated by VIGAS have a real and transformative implications for the deployment of robots. VIGAS effectively creates a new Pareto frontier, where there is no longer a trade-off in which control performance is traded-off for computational efficiency, and thus sampling-based control may become viable for embodied robot applications that are resource-constrained. This unprecedented efficiency enables two important capabilities; first, a potential for control frequencies that are much higher, leading to stable and reactive behaviors, and second, the ability to plan over greater horizons, leading to complex, near-and-far-sighted behaviors. The sweeping nature of the advances suggests an even broader form of the benefit, which should not be constrained to locomotion, indeed is applicable to general robotics control problems that exhibit high-dimensional, nonlinear dynamics.

While tem-porally correlated noise can improve control smoothness, the task-dependent and sometimes catastrophic variation in performance, also suggests that fixed-exploration strategies may be brittle, which is a validation of our primary hypothesis of the necessity of adaptive exploration strategies for robust, general purpose MPC perfor-mance. 

# 7.3 Limitations of the Current Work 

Despite its demonstrated advantages, our work has several limitations that warrant acknowledgment and suggest important directions for improvement. 

• Computational Overhead per Update: While VIGAS achieves overall speedups through sample efficiency, its per-update cost is higher than DIAL-MPC due to SVD computation ( O(N D min (N, D )) complexity). For scenarios requiring extremely large sample sizes or deployment on hardware without optimized linear algebra routines, this could become a bottleneck. 

• Unimodal Gaussian Limitation: The variational model assumes a single multivariate Gaussian distribution, which may inadequately represent fun-damentally multi-modal reward landscapes with multiple distinct optimal solutions. In such cases, VIGAS might converge to one mode while ignoring others or produce suboptimal compromises by averaging between modes. 

• Hyperparameter Sensitivity: While VIGAS eliminates annealing schedules, it introduces its own hyperparameters: rank k, initial variance vigas_init_var ,temperature λ, EMA learning rates ( αμ, α cov ), and covariance floor 

vigas_cov_floor . Although our experiments demonstrate robust performance across tasks, tuning remains a task-specific process requiring domain expertise. 

• Simulation-to-Reality Gap: All evaluations were conducted in the Brax physics simulator. Real-world deployment faces additional challenges including sensor noise, actuator delays, model uncertainties, and communication latencies that may affect performance characteristics. 

# 7.4 Future Research Directions 

The demonstrated potential of VIGAS, combined with its current limitations, sug-gests several exciting avenues for future research that could significantly advance the field.

• Automated Hyperparameter Adaptation: Develop meta-learning or Bayesian optimization frameworks [23] for automatic hyperparameter tuning. A particularly promising direction is learning to adapt rank k and learning rates online based on convergence patterns, moving toward truly autonomous controller configuration.

This thesis has demonstrated that adaptive, variational methods represent a signifi-cant advancement in real-time robotic control, fundamentally changing the landscape of sampling-based MPC. By establishing a new paradigm where exploration strategies emerge from data rather than predetermined heuristics, VIGAS opens rich research directions that bridge classical control theory with modern machine learning. The transformation from competing objective, high performance versus computa-tional efficiency, to complementary advantages represents more than an incremental improvement. It suggests a fundamental rethinking of how we approach complex control problems, moving from hand-crafted solutions toward adaptive, intelligent systems that learn and improve their strategies online. While the journey from simulation to real-world deployment remains the ultimate validation, our comprehensive evaluation provides compelling evidence that VIGAS represents a transformative step toward making sophisticated sampling-based control a practical reality for complex robotic systems. The principles, methods, and insights developed in this work establish a foundation for future research at the exciting intersection of optimal control and machine learning, promising more intelligent, efficient, and capable autonomous robots. The future of robotic control lies not in more sophisticated fixed strategies, but in systems that can adapt, learn, and improve their exploration approaches based on the unique characteristics of each problem they encounter. VIGAS represents an important step toward that future. 47
